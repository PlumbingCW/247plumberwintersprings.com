Non-Condensing Tankless Water Heater: What It Is and How It Vents

By the Winter Springs 24/7 Plumbing team

Plumber on call, 24/7
(407) 964-8940Call Now
Short answer

A non-condensing tankless water heater is a gas on-demand unit with one heat exchanger. Its exhaust leaves hot, so it needs heat-resistant vent material, and it normally has no acidic condensate to drain. A condensing unit adds a second heat exchanger that pulls more heat from the exhaust, which cools it, and that cooler exhaust makes condensate that must drain away.

The word condensing sounds like a technical footnote, but it decides your vent material, your drain needs, and part of your install scope. Two units that look alike on a wall can need very different work behind them.

Below is how each type works, how they compare, and what to ask before you sign. For equipment options, see our tankless water heater page, and for the installation side, the water heater installation page covers the planning.

Non-condensing versus condensing at a glance

Both heat water the same way at the burner. The difference is what happens to the exhaust.

FeatureNon-condensingCondensing
Heat exchangersOneTwo, the second captures exhaust heat
Exhaust temperatureHotMuch cooler
Vent materialHeat-resistant, such as stainless steel, as the maker specifiesOften plastic types the maker approves
CondensateLittle to none in normal useAcidic liquid that needs a drain, often with a neutralizer
EfficiencyLowerHigher
Typical upfront costOften lower for the unitOften higher for the unit, with extra drain work

Always follow the installation manual for the exact model. Vent rules are set by the maker.

How the non-condensing design works

When you open a hot tap, flow sensors tell the burner to fire. The burner heats a single metal heat exchanger, and water passing through picks up the heat on the spot. Combustion gases then rise out the vent.

Because those gases leave while still hot, they stay as vapor and do not condense inside the vent. That is why the vent has to survive high temperatures, and why an ordinary plastic pipe is not acceptable for this design.

Why venting is the heart of the project

Venting is where tankless installs most often go wrong. The vent must be the right material, the right diameter, within the allowed length, and sealed at every joint. A leaking vent can push carbon monoxide into the home.

The maker's manual lists the allowed vent lengths and fittings for each model. Every elbow adds resistance, so a long, bendy route can force you to a different unit or a different location. Do not substitute parts. Mixing vent products from different makers is a common mistake.

Which type fits which home

Neither design is better in every house.

Replacing an older vent run

A home with an existing metal flue may suit a non-condensing unit, if the flue is sound and approved for the new model.

No convenient drain nearby

Condensing units need a condensate path. A non-condensing unit avoids that if the drain is far from the mounting spot.

High use and long ownership

Condensing units use fuel more efficiently, so they suit homes with heavy hot water use and plans to stay put.

Tight budget on the unit

A non-condensing model is often less expensive to buy, though venting can erase the gap.

What condensate is and why it matters

When exhaust cools below a certain point, water vapor in it turns to liquid. In a condensing unit that happens on purpose inside the second heat exchanger, and the liquid is mildly acidic.

That liquid must leave through a drain line, usually to a floor drain, a laundry standpipe, or a condensate pump. Many installs add a neutralizer cartridge so the acid does not attack drain pipe. A blocked condensate line can shut a condensing unit down, so keep the route clear and visible.

Replacing a tank with tankless: what changes at the vent

A tank heater often shares a vent with a furnace or sits under a draft hood. A tankless unit usually has its own sealed route, and may pull combustion air from inside or from outside depending on the model.

That means a hole through a wall or roof, a new termination point that must stay clear of windows and air intakes, and clearances from combustibles. These are manual requirements, not preferences. A plumber who has installed the model can tell you quickly if your wall or attic allows it.

Questions to ask the installer

Good answers here predict a clean install.

  • ✓Is this unit condensing or non-condensing
  • ✓What vent material does the manual require
  • ✓How long is the vent run and how many elbows
  • ✓Where will condensate drain, if the unit makes it
  • ✓Is the gas line sized for the unit's rated input
  • ✓Who handles the permit questions with the city
  • ✓What flush schedule do you recommend for hard water

Reading your own situation

If you already own a tankless unit, the model plate on its side shows whether it is condensing, and the vent pipe is a clue. Metal vent usually points to a non-condensing unit, while white plastic vent often points to a condensing one. The manual is the final word.

If a unit was installed by someone else and the vent looks improvised, loose, or discolored, have a licensed plumber inspect it. Brown staining around a joint can mean exhaust is escaping. Treat that as a safety issue and do not wait for the next service.

Why efficiency is not the only factor

Condensing units win on paper because they capture more heat. But a unit that cannot be vented or drained correctly in your home is the wrong unit, however efficient. Match the design to the house first, then compare running costs on your actual usage.

A mid-range model that fits the vent route and gas line beats a premium one that forces a long, bent, or improvised vent.

What a careful install looks like

The sequence matters for safety.

  1. 1

    Confirm the model and vent type

    Match the unit to the vent material and route you can actually build in your home, using the maker's tables.

  2. 2

    Check gas supply and shutoff

    The pipe must supply the unit's rated input at full firing. The plumber installs a shutoff valve and a drip leg or union as the manual requires.

  3. 3

    Run and seal the vent

    Each joint is assembled per the manual, supported, and sloped as specified.

  4. 4

    Connect water and set service valves

    Isolation valves on the hot and cold lines let you flush the unit later without cutting pipe.

  5. 5

    Test for leaks and combustion

    The plumber checks all gas joints for leaks, runs the unit, and verifies it fires and vents properly.

If you smell gas

Leave the building right away and call 911 or your gas utility from outside. Do not flip switches or look for the source. Call a plumber only after the utility says it is safe.

Electric tankless is a different animal

Electric tankless units have no burner, no vent, and no condensate. They do draw heavy current, so the panel and wiring decide whether one fits. The terms condensing and non-condensing apply only to gas units.

If your home has no gas or no practical vent path, an electric model may be worth asking about, with an electrician checking the load.

Maintenance and what to expect

Both types need periodic flushing to clear scale, and a visual check of the vent and condensate line. In a condensing unit, the neutralizer cartridge must be checked and refilled per the manual.

If a unit shows an error code, do not keep resetting it. Repeated lockouts can mean a blocked vent, a gas supply problem, or scale. Turn the unit off and call a plumber. Property managers should log the install date and the vent type for future vendors.
