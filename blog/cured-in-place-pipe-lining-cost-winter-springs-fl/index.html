What Drives the Cost of Cured-in-Place Pipe Lining

By the Winter Springs 24/7 Plumbing team

Plumber on call, 24/7
(407) 964-8940Call Now
Short answer

The cost of cured-in-place pipe lining depends on how much pipe is lined, the pipe diameter, how many branch connections must be reopened, what shape the old pipe is in, and how hard the work area is to reach. Prep work such as cleaning, root cutting and camera inspection also counts. Because those facts differ at every house, a reliable price comes only after a camera inspection of your line.

Cured-in-place pipe lining, often called CIPP, repairs a damaged sewer line from the inside. A flexible liner soaked in resin is pulled into the old pipe, inflated, and hardened. The result is a new pipe within the old one, without a long trench through your yard.

Homeowners ask about cost first, but cost follows condition. This article explains what moves the price, what the process involves, and when lining is the wrong tool.

How the liner goes in

The crew starts by cleaning the line. Roots, scale and grease come out with a cutting head or a water jet, because the liner needs a clean pipe wall to bond to. A camera then confirms the cleanup and measures the length.

The liner is a felt tube coated with epoxy resin. It is pulled or inverted into the pipe and pushed against the wall with air or water pressure. Heat or UV light then cures the resin into a hard shell.

Last, a cutting robot reopens any branch connections, and a final camera run checks the finished pipe. The camera recording is your proof that the work holds up.

Six things that move the price

No honest article can give a single figure for lining. Here are the real drivers, in roughly the order they matter.

Length of the lined section

More pipe means more liner, more resin, more curing time and a longer day on site.

Pipe diameter

A larger pipe needs a thicker, larger liner. Most home laterals are small, but a main line serving several fixtures may differ.

Number of connections

Every branch that joins the line must be found and reopened with a cutter. More branches mean more work.

Condition of the old pipe

Heavy root damage, offsets or sagging sections need prep or point repairs before lining can start.

Access and cleanouts

A good cleanout makes entry simple. If one must be installed, that adds a step.

Curing method and site setup

Hot water, steam and UV curing differ in equipment and time. Keeping water use down during the cure matters too.

Insurance note

Insurance typically covers sudden accidental damage, not wear or the repair of the failed pipe itself. Roots and age are usually wear. Ask your insurer what applies to your policy before work starts.

Our plumbers give a clear price before work starts, and it follows what the camera showed, not a flat guess.

Signs your line may be a lining candidate

Lining fits best when the pipe is damaged but still in place. Typical triggers are repeated root clogs in the same spot, a camera view of hairline cracks, or joints that have opened slightly.

Repeated backups that clear after cleaning and return within months point the same way. The cleaning proves the line can pass water, but something keeps catching debris. A camera shows whether a liner would cover that spot.

A sewer smell in the yard, or a damp patch above the line, can mean a leak in the pipe wall. That may also be repairable by lining, if the pipe shape allows.

Why the inspection comes before any price

A sewer inspection is the step that turns guesses into numbers. The camera shows the pipe length, the number of branches, the material and the damage. Without it, a lining quote is a coin toss.

Ask to see the recording. You should be able to point to the crack, the root mass or the offset and understand why the plan fits. If a company will not show the footage, find another company.

The camera also shows when lining will not work. A pipe that has collapsed cannot be relined, and a good plumber will say so before taking your deposit.

Limits worth knowing

Lining narrows the pipe slightly because the liner has wall thickness. In most cases flow is still fine, since the new surface is smooth, but a very small pipe can feel the loss.

Lining does not fix a bad slope. A belly that holds water will still hold water after lining. And lining cannot be done in a line that has not been cleared. If the camera cannot pass, the prep must come first.

Ways to keep the job simple and the price clear

Locate your cleanout before the crew arrives. A clear, reachable cleanout saves time and avoids an extra access step.

Keep a record of past backups, including dates and which fixtures were affected. That history helps the plumber target the right section.

Ask for the price to be split into inspection, prep, lining and restoration. A split price lets you compare offers fairly and see which parts are fixed and which depend on what the crew finds.

Comparing lining with a traditional dig

A traditional dig opens a trench along the pipe and replaces it section by section. It handles collapsed pipe and bad slope, and it gives you a brand new line. It also tears up lawn, driveways and landscaping.

Lining leaves most of the yard alone, since the crew works from the cleanout and a small access point. It often takes less time on site. The trade-off is that it relies on the old pipe being reasonably aligned.

Cost between the two depends on depth, surface restoration and length. A deep line under a driveway can make lining the cheaper option. A short shallow line in open lawn can swing the other way.

What to ask before you sign

Ask who inspects the line and whether you receive the video. Ask what happens if the camera finds a collapsed section partway through. Ask how branch connections are reopened and checked.

Ask what the price includes, from cleaning and root cutting to the final camera run. Ask whether any permit step applies, and who handles it. Get all of it in writing.
