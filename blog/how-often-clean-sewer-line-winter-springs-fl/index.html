How Often Should You Clean the Sewer Line?

By the Winter Springs 24/7 Plumbing team

Plumber on call, 24/7
(407) 964-8940Call Now
Short answer

A sewer line with no history of trouble does not need cleaning on a schedule. Clean it when symptoms appear: slow drains in more than one fixture, gurgling, repeat clogs, or sewage odors. A line with roots or a known sag may need cleaning every year or two. A camera inspection after the first cleaning shows which group your house falls into.

Search for this question and you will find advice from every direction. Some say every year, others say every five. Neither is useful, because a sewer line is not a filter you swap on a date.

What matters is what is happening inside your pipe. A smooth, well-sloped line can run for decades with little attention. A line with roots, grease or a belly can back up in months. The better question is how to tell which kind you have.

Why a calendar does not work

Sewer lines clog for physical reasons. Roots grow into joints looking for moisture. Grease cools and hardens on pipe walls. Wipes and paper towels catch on rough spots. A low section, called a belly, holds standing water that collects debris.

None of these follow a calendar. A tree root may take five years to block a pipe or one wet season. A line that was clear last spring can still be half full of roots by fall.

That is why a good plumber asks about your history first. How many backups have you had, and where did they appear? Those answers say more than the date of your last cleaning.

Signs the line needs attention now

Several fixtures acting up together is the clearest sign. If the shower gurgles when the washer drains, or a toilet bubbles when you run the tub, the shared main line is struggling to carry water.

A sewage smell near a floor drain or in the yard points the same way. So does a patch of grass that is greener or soggier than the rest. Water that stays in the bowl after you flush is another clue.

These signs call for action within days, not weeks. A partial clog can turn into a full backup at the worst time, usually when the whole family is home and the laundry is running.

Reading the signs of roots versus grease

The pattern of your clogs hints at the cause. Roots tend to cause slow, steady decline, with gurgling that gets worse over months. Grease tends to cause trouble in the kitchen first and shows up as repeated sink backups.

A sudden full stoppage after normal use often means a collapsed joint or a wad of wipes. These are guesses, not a diagnosis. Only a camera confirms which one you have, and the answer changes how often cleaning pays off.

What to do when a drain slows down

Work through these in order. They separate a simple branch clog from a main line problem.

  1. 1

    Test other fixtures

    Run a sink, flush a toilet and watch the lowest drain in the house. Water backing up in the lowest fixture means the main line may be the problem.

  2. 2

    Stop heavy water use

    Hold off on laundry and long showers. More water into a blocked line only pushes sewage up through the lowest opening.

  3. 3

    Find the cleanout

    Many homes have a capped cleanout pipe near the foundation or in the yard. Knowing where it is saves time when help arrives. Do not open it if water is rising, since sewage can spill.

  4. 4

    Call for cleaning and a camera

    A cable machine or water jet clears the line, and a camera run shows what caused the problem. The video guides how often you should repeat it.

What cleaning actually does

A professional sewer line cleaning starts with access at the cleanout. A cable machine with a cutting head chews through roots and soft blockages. A hydro jet uses high pressure water to scrape grease from the pipe walls.

The tool depends on the cause. Roots usually need a cutting head. Grease responds best to the jet. Paper and wipes clear with either.

Cleaning restores flow but does not repair a cracked or sagging pipe. If the camera finds broken pipe, the same blockage will return until the damaged section is fixed.

What goes down the drain matters

Grease is the quiet cause behind many backups. It leaves the kitchen as warm liquid and sticks to the cold pipe wall a few feet later. Layer after layer builds a ring that narrows the pipe until paper catches on it.

Wipes are the other offender. Even those sold as flushable do not break down like toilet paper, and they knit together with grease and roots into a solid mat. The mat holds back everything behind it.

Collect cooking grease in a can and throw it away with the trash. Keep wipes, paper towels and feminine products out of toilets. Those two habits do more for sewer health than any cleaning schedule.

Why the camera is worth the extra step

Many homeowners skip the camera to save time. That often costs more later. Without a look inside, nobody knows whether roots, grease or a collapsed joint caused the clog.

A sewer inspection records the pipe from the cleanout to the connection. You see the slope, the joints and any offsets. The recording becomes your baseline, and a repeat inspection years later shows what has changed.

A camera also has limits. It cannot see through a full blockage, which is why cleaning often comes first. It also cannot measure pipe strength, only what is visible on the pipe wall.

When cleaning is not enough

If the line backs up again within weeks of cleaning, the cause is probably structural. A crushed section, a joint pushed apart by roots, or a belly that holds water will keep catching debris.

At that point more cleaning only buys time. The camera shows the damage, and a repair or lining plan stops the cycle. Ask for the recording so you can see the problem yourself.

Setting a maintenance interval that makes sense

Once the camera has shown the line, the interval follows. A clean pipe with no roots needs nothing but attention to symptoms. A pipe with light root growth may need cleaning every year or two. A pipe with heavy grease or an uneven slope may need it more often until it is repaired.

Habits help. Keep grease and wipes out of drains, even products labeled flushable. Run plenty of water after laundry. Keep trees with aggressive roots away from the sewer path when you can.
