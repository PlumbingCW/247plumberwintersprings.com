Wet ceiling, soft floor, or a water bill that makes no sense

A hidden leak often starts with a symptom and no obvious source. A brown ring on drywall, a floor that feels spongy by the dishwasher, or a meter that keeps turning with every tap closed all mean water is going somewhere it should not.

Leak detection narrows it down in steps. We close every fixture, read the meter, then pressure test the supply line to learn whether the leak is on the supply side or in a drain. Acoustic listening and a thermal camera then trace the spot without opening walls at random.

A camera cannot see through a slab, so some results come back as a likely zone rather than one exact point. We tell you which one you have before anyone cuts a hole, and we say what each result does and does not prove.

When the water heater is the thing leaking

A heater that weeps at the base, trips a breaker or runs out of hot water fast deserves a prompt call. Look at where the water sits. A drip from the relief valve pipe, a wet fitting on top and a rusted seam on the bottom of the tank each mean something different.

A loose fitting or a failed valve is a repair. A seam leak is not, because the steel itself has given up. We explain which one you have, then handle water heater repair or tell you plainly that replacement is the better route.

Chloramine in Oviedo water and the rubber parts it ages

According to the city's 2024 water report, Oviedo draws from 10 Floridan wells, uses forced draft aeration and disinfects with chloramine. Chloramine stays active in pipes longer than plain chlorine, which helps keep water safe on the way to your tap.

It can also wear down rubber, including toilet flappers, washing machine hoses and faucet washers. A toilet that keeps running or a hose that feels soft is often a worn rubber part, not a pipe problem. Replace bulging hoses before they split, and see toilet repair if the tank will not stop refilling.
