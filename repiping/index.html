Repiping in Winter Springs, FL

Brown water on first draw, weak showers or a second pinhole leak in a year. Old supply pipe tends to send these warnings before it fails.

Repiping in Winter Springs, FL means replacing the water supply pipes inside a house, either the whole system or a major section, with new PEX or copper. It is a planned job, usually done when old pipe keeps leaking or no longer delivers clean, steady water.

This page explains the warning signs, how we decide between a patch and a full repipe, and what to expect in your home during the work.

Rusty water, low pressure and repeat leaks: when old pipe is done

One leak does not mean a house needs new pipe. A pattern does.

Brown or orange water

Rust flakes inside galvanized steel pipe color the water, especially after it sits overnight.

Low pressure at several fixtures

Mineral and rust buildup narrows the inside of the pipe until flow drops.

Pinhole leaks that keep coming

Repairing one pinhole in copper often precedes another, because the pipe wall is thin along its length.

Stains on ceilings and walls

Slow leaks inside walls leave spreading stains, and the damp framing can grow mold.

Metallic taste or odor

Corroded pipe can affect taste. If you are worried about water quality, a lab test answers it, and we do not give medical advice.

PEX or copper: which pipe goes in

Most repipes today use PEX or copper. Both work well when installed correctly, and the choice comes down to the house and your priorities.

Galvanized pipe, CPVC and the case for replacing

Galvanized steel pipe has a zinc coating that wears off. Rust forms inside, slowly narrowing the passage and giving the water an orange tint. Replacing it is usually a matter of when, not whether.

CPVC is the cream-colored plastic pipe used in many homes from later decades. It can turn brittle with age and heat, and fittings are often where it cracks. A single crack does not mean the whole house needs replacing, but several do.

We look at the pipe you actually have, test it and tell you plainly whether repair or replacement is the better call.

The City of Winter Springs reports that its water averages 150 mg/L hardness, which it lists as 8 grains. On the USGS scale, 121 to 180 mg/L counts as hard.

Hardness scale builds on heating elements and clogs aerators, and it can also line pipe walls over the years. That is general mineral behavior, not a claim about your pipes. If you plan a repipe, a water softener is worth discussing alongside it.

How a whole-house repipe is done

A repipe disrupts the house for a short time, but it follows a clear order.

  1. 1

    Walk-through and plan

    We trace the current layout, mark fixtures and choose pipe routes through the attic, walls and floor.

  2. 2

    Shut off and drain

    We close the main, drain the system and set up so you can still have water at points where possible.

  3. 3

    Open access points

    We cut small openings to fish the new pipe, instead of removing whole walls.

  4. 4

    Run and connect new lines

    New supply lines connect to every fixture, the water heater and the main.

  5. 5

    Pressure test and patch

    We test every connection at full pressure before closing openings. Wall patching is arranged separately.

Repiping, slab leaks and spot repairs

Sometimes repiping is the answer to a leak under the floor. If the lines that run beneath the concrete have failed more than once, rerouting new pipe through the attic or walls avoids breaking the slab each time. Read about that decision on our slab leak repair page.

If your problem is a single burst or one clear break, we will repair it rather than recommend a full job. Burst pipe repair is the faster fix when the rest of the pipe is sound.

Getting the house ready for a repipe

A little preparation keeps the work quick and your belongings clean.

  • ✓Clear the areas under sinks and around the water heater.
  • ✓Move furniture away from walls where we will open access.
  • ✓Empty closets that sit on a pipe route.
  • ✓Plan for the water to be off for part of the day.
  • ✓Secure pets away from open walls and tools.
  • ✓Take photos of walls and ceilings before work begins.

Mixing old and new pipe

A partial repipe connects new pipe to old, and that joint is where problems start if dissimilar metals touch. We use dielectric fittings or transition couplings to avoid galvanic corrosion where copper meets steel.

Partial work makes sense when only one branch is bad, like a bathroom group or a single run to the kitchen. The weak point is that other old lines can still fail later. We tell you that trade-off before you choose.

What drives repiping cost

We give a clear price before work starts. A repipe is not a flat-rate job, because these factors vary from house to house.

Size of the house and fixture count

More bathrooms and fixtures mean more pipe and more connections.

Pipe material

PEX and copper differ in material cost and labor.

Access

An open attic is easier than a finished two-story home with tile and cabinets in the way.

Whole house or partial

Replacing one section costs less than all supply lines.

Patching and finishing

Drywall repair, paint and flooring are separate work after the plumbing is done.

Insurance note

If an old pipe bursts and ruins a floor, insurance typically looks at that sudden accidental damage. Replacing pipe because it is old, worn or corroded is a planned upgrade, not an accident. Ask your insurer before you decide.
